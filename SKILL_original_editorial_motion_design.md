# ORIGINAL EDITORIAL MOTION DESIGN

## Skill for premium, concept-led motion graphics

## 1. Mission

Create motion graphics that feel art-directed, materially convincing,
rhythmically controlled, and narratively purposeful. The goal is not to
reproduce a particular video, template, creator, channel, or
recognizable sequence. The goal is to apply transferable design
principles---material coherence, visual hierarchy, meaningful camera
movement, restrained easing, tactile surfaces, contrast, and visual
storytelling---to a **new visual identity designed specifically for the
user's subject**.

This skill applies to editorial explainers, documentary motion design,
cultural films, typography-led pieces, educational films, brand films,
and mixed-media animation. It can be implemented in After Effects,
Blender, HTML Canvas, SVG, CSS, JavaScript, or AI video tools.

### Success criteria

A successful result must: - Communicate one clear idea at a time. - Have
an original concept and visual metaphor suited to the subject. - Use
motion to reveal, connect, compare, explain, or transform information. -
Maintain a consistent material, lighting, color, typography, and depth
logic. - Use easing and timing that feel deliberate rather than
mechanically linear. - Preserve readability and give important ideas
enough screen time. - Avoid generic AI-video aesthetics, gratuitous
effects, and decorative motion without narrative purpose. - Be
reproducible from explicit scene instructions rather than vague style
adjectives.

## 2. Non-negotiable originality rule

**Extract principles; do not copy a source's expression.**

When a reference is supplied or discussed: 1. Identify general
techniques such as layered depth, tactile material treatment, restrained
camera moves, contrast, pacing, or progressive reveal. 2. Do not reuse
the reference's exact sequence of shots, compositions, subject
arrangement, iconic props, distinctive metaphor, color placement, text
layout, or transition choreography unless the user explicitly owns those
assets and requests a faithful recreation. 3. Design a new visual
metaphor, composition system, movement motif, palette, and scene
progression for the current topic. 4. Do not mention or name an
unrequested reference in the finished creative brief, prompt,
storyboard, or agent-facing instructions. 5. Never use another work's
identity as a substitute for understanding the user's subject. 6. If the
user asks for "the same level," interpret this as the same level of
craft, clarity, material believability, motion control, and art
direction---not the same look or scene. 7. Run an originality check
before delivery: could this piece be mistaken for a shot-by-shot remake?
If yes, redesign its signature visual idea and staging.

### What can transfer

-   A reveal can move from detail to context.
-   Depth can be built from scale, overlap, shadow, perspective, and
    parallax.
-   Materials can have coherent surface texture and edge behavior.
-   A key idea can receive visual priority through contrast and timing.
-   Transitions can be motivated by shape, movement, meaning, or spatial
    continuity.
-   Easing can be restrained and responsive to the perceived weight of
    each object.

### What must be reinvented per project

-   The central metaphor.
-   The hero object or visual symbol.
-   The scene order and composition.
-   The color palette and accent-color logic.
-   The shapes, textures, and typography treatment.
-   The movement signature and transition language.
-   Any distinctive motif associated with an existing work.

## 3. Start with meaning, not effects

Before designing, establish:

-   **Subject:** What is the film about?
-   **Audience:** Who must understand or feel it?
-   **Core message:** Express the central idea in one sentence.
-   **Emotional arc:** What should the viewer feel at the beginning,
    middle, and end?
-   **Visual thesis:** What single visual idea could communicate the
    subject without relying entirely on narration?
-   **Information hierarchy:** What is primary, secondary, and
    supporting?
-   **Format:** Duration, aspect ratio, resolution, frame rate,
    language, platform, and safe areas.
-   **Production method:** Code-driven animation, compositing, 2D, 2.5D,
    3D, AI-generated footage, or a hybrid.
-   **Constraints:** Exact wording, logos, supplied assets,
    accessibility, legal/brand restrictions, and assets that must remain
    unchanged.

If essential details are missing, make reasonable assumptions and label
them. Ask only questions that materially change the creative result. Do
not block progress over details that can safely be decided by art
direction.

## 4. Build a project-specific visual DNA

Create a compact visual system before making scenes.

### 4.1 Material logic

Choose materials because they support the subject, not because a preset
looks attractive. Possible material families include paper, ink, fabric,
wood, metal, glass, stone, printed ephemera, photographic grain,
technical diagrams, clay, or clean vector surfaces.

For every material, define: - Base color and tonal range. - Surface
detail: fibers, grain, pores, scratches, speckles, print wear, or
smoothness. - Edge behavior: cut, torn, folded, beveled, frayed, or
crisp. - Light response: diffuse, matte, glossy, metallic, translucent,
or emissive. - Shadow behavior and perceived thickness. - Wear level and
scale of texture. - How the material relates to the other materials.

**Material coherence rule:** all materials must appear to inhabit the
same designed world. Do not add texture indiscriminately. A texture must
have a plausible scale, contrast, and reason to exist.

### 4.2 Color system

Define roles, not just hex values: - **Base:** establishes the
environment. - **Surface:** carries the primary content. - **Ink/type:**
provides legibility. - **Accent:** identifies a key concept or action. -
**Highlight:** draws attention to a focal point. - **Shadow/depth:**
separates layers without muddying colors.

Use a restrained palette by default, but do not force an antique or
paper-based palette onto subjects that need a different visual language.
Accent colors should have a clear semantic or compositional role. Check
contrast at actual output size and on mobile screens.

### 4.3 Typography system

Define: - Primary typeface or lettering approach. - Secondary
typeface. - Hierarchy of title, body, labels, annotations, and
captions. - Alignment, line length, spacing, and safe margins. - Whether
type is a flat graphic, a physical printed object, engraved lettering,
projected light, or another material. - Entrance, hold, and exit
behavior.

Text is content, not decoration. Preserve exact user-supplied wording.
Do not invent, garble, mirror, or distort language. For Arabic, verify
right-to-left order, connected letterforms, diacritics when required,
and correct shaping. Avoid animating individual letters in ways that
break legibility unless the concept specifically requires it.

### 4.4 Shape and composition language

Choose a consistent family of shapes: organic, geometric, archival,
architectural, hand-cut, technical, typographic, or hybrid. Define how
elements are aligned, overlapped, cropped, framed, and grouped.

Avoid copying a reference's distinctive arrangement. Build a new
composition grammar appropriate to the subject.

### 4.5 Light and depth

Define the light direction, softness, contrast, ambient level, shadow
length, and whether the environment is flat, layered, or volumetric.
Even a fully 2D piece benefits from a consistent light logic if it
depicts physical objects.

Depth can be communicated through: - Overlap and occlusion. - Scale
differences. - Perspective and convergence. - Shadow and contact
shadow. - Tonal separation. - Parallax. - Focus or detail hierarchy,
used sparingly.

Do not claim that a scene is physically 3D merely because it looks
dimensional. Choose 2D, 2.5D, or 3D according to the shot's needs and
the production budget.

## 5. Design a narrative movement system

Every movement must have a job. Classify it before animating:

-   **Reveal:** expose information that was hidden or cropped.
-   **Focus:** guide attention toward a specific object or detail.
-   **Connect:** show that two ideas belong together.
-   **Compare:** align or contrast information.
-   **Transform:** make a conceptual change visible.
-   **Travel:** move through a designed environment.
-   **Emphasize:** add controlled force to a key moment.
-   **Resolve:** settle the scene into a readable final state.

If a movement has no clear job, remove it or redesign it.

### 5.1 Camera movement

Use a limited, motivated set of camera behaviors: - Push-in: increase
intimacy or importance. - Pull-back: reveal context or scale. - Pan:
traverse a designed field or connect adjacent information. - Tilt:
reveal vertical relationships. - Truck/dolly: create lateral parallax or
move through layers. - Orbit: reveal form only when spatial
understanding benefits. - Static hold: protect readability and let a key
idea land.

Do not move the camera continuously by default. A still camera is often
the most professional choice when the content needs reading.

### 5.2 Movement continuity

When moving from one scene to another, establish a relationship: - Match
a shape, direction, scale, color, or gesture. - Let one object become
the next scene's organizing element. - Use a motivated wipe, occlusion,
crop, or spatial move. - Cut deliberately when continuity would weaken
the idea.

Do not force every transition to be seamless. A purposeful hard cut can
be stronger than an elaborate transition.

### 5.3 Easing and perceived weight

Use easing appropriate to the object and intention: - Large camera
moves: gradual acceleration/deceleration. - UI-like or precise elements:
controlled, clean settling. - Heavy objects: restrained starts/stops and
believable momentum. - Light paper or small graphic pieces: subtle
movement, never automatic exaggerated bounce. - Text: prioritize
legibility and controlled settling. - Hero elements: clear arrival
followed by a readable hold.

Avoid applying the same bounce, elastic curve, overshoot, or speed
profile to every layer. Do not confuse "smooth" with "slow." Motion
should be responsive, intentional, and varied according to purpose.

### 5.4 Motion hierarchy

At any moment, decide which element leads. Usually: 1. One primary
movement. 2. Optional secondary movement that supports it. 3. Very
restrained ambient movement, only if it improves the scene.

Do not animate every layer simultaneously. Secondary motion should never
compete with the main action or make text hard to read.

### 5.5 Holds and readability

Plan explicit holds after important reveals. Viewers need time to
recognize an image, read a title, understand a comparison, or absorb a
metaphor. A movement is not complete when the object reaches its
position; it is complete when the audience has had enough time to
understand it.

## 6. Build tactile materials without making the result look fake

For physical-looking elements, evaluate these dimensions separately:

### Surface

Use texture at the right scale. Fine fibers should not look like large
scratches; grain should not overwhelm small text. Avoid repeating
obvious texture tiles.

### Edges

Edges communicate material and manufacturing: - Paper may be torn,
folded, cut, curled, or layered. - Metal may have crisp edges and
controlled highlights. - Fabric may have fibers and soft folds. -
Printed material may show ink variation or slight misregistration. -
Vector graphics may remain clean if that is the chosen design language.

Do not add torn edges to every object. Material behavior must vary
according to the chosen world.

### Contact and cast shadows

A contact shadow anchors an object to a surface. A cast shadow
communicates height and light direction. Keep them consistent with the
scene's lighting. Avoid identical, dark, oversized shadows beneath every
layer.

### Imperfection

Use controlled variation, not random damage. Wear, scratches, stains,
grain, and irregularity must support the subject and scale. "More
texture" does not automatically mean "more realism."

### Highlights and metallic surfaces

Metal requires internal tonal variation and coherent highlight behavior,
not simply a yellow fill or strong glow. Use glows only when motivated
by a real light source or a deliberate graphic convention.

### Integration

All objects must share compatible sharpness, contrast, lighting, color
treatment, and grain. A photorealistic object pasted into a flat
collage---or a perfectly clean vector on a heavily distressed
surface---needs deliberate justification or integration work.

## 7. Backgrounds are active design elements

A background may establish a world, provide information, create depth,
or reduce competition around a focal element.

Choose one principal role per scene: - **Information-rich field:**
diagrams, documents, maps, collections, or interconnected elements. -
**Neutral stage:** low-detail background for a product, title, or hero
object. - **Spatial environment:** a place through which the camera
travels. - **Graphic field:** abstract shapes and color used to organize
information. - **Transition field:** a temporary visual bridge between
scenes.

### Density control

Vary density deliberately across the film. Busy scenes need a clear
focal hierarchy; sparse scenes need strong composition and enough visual
intention to avoid feeling empty.

### Contrast control

To emphasize an object, do not automatically brighten it. Alternatives
include reducing background contrast, simplifying nearby shapes, using
negative space, changing scale, or delaying secondary motion.

### Background continuity

Keep material and light logic consistent unless a scene change is meant
to signal a meaningful shift. Do not carry the same background into
every shot simply for consistency; continuity should support the story,
not flatten it.

## 8. Editorial composition and visual hierarchy

Use a clear hierarchy: - **Primary:** what the viewer should notice
first. - **Secondary:** information that explains or supports the
primary. - **Tertiary:** texture, atmosphere, decoration, or contextual
detail.

Establish hierarchy through scale, contrast, position, isolation,
timing, motion, and negative space---not scale alone.

### Composition checks

-   Is the focal point clear within one second?
-   Does the eye know where to go next?
-   Are important objects cropped intentionally?
-   Are margins and safe areas respected?
-   Do elements overlap for a reason?
-   Is there enough negative space around text?
-   Is the composition balanced without becoming rigid?
-   Does the frame still work at the intended display size?

Use asymmetry when it creates direction or energy. Use symmetry when it
reinforces authority, clarity, or ritual. Do not use a single
composition formula for every project.

## 9. Scene construction workflow

Follow this sequence for every new motion-graphics project.

### Step 1 --- Brief extraction

Write a short brief containing: - Subject and audience. - One-sentence
message. - Emotional arc. - Duration and output format. - Exact text and
required assets. - Technical and brand constraints. - Chosen production
method.

### Step 2 --- Concept exploration

Create at least three distinct visual concepts when the task is
open-ended. They should differ in visual metaphor and composition, not
merely color. Select the concept that best communicates the idea, not
the one with the most effects.

### Step 3 --- Visual DNA sheet

Document: - Palette and color roles. - Material family and edge
behavior. - Typography hierarchy. - Lighting and depth. - Shape
language. - Motion principles. - Rules for text, logos, and recurring
assets.

### Step 4 --- Beat sheet

Break the duration into meaningful narrative beats. For each beat,
define: - What the viewer learns or feels. - The dominant visual. - The
main movement. - The information hierarchy. - How the beat leads into
the next one.

Do not decide shot duration by evenly dividing the runtime. Give each
idea the time it needs.

### Step 5 --- Storyboard

Create clear frames that show composition and visual progression.
Include enough frames to communicate the actual transitions and major
changes. A storyboard is not a decorative mood board: it must explain
what changes from one beat to the next.

### Step 6 --- Asset and layer plan

List the required assets, their source, format, resolution, and whether
they must remain editable. Separate foreground, midground, background,
shadows, texture, typography, and effects when that supports production.

### Step 7 --- Motion specification

For every shot, specify: - Duration. - Starting state. - Main action. -
Camera behavior. - Easing/settling behavior. - Secondary motion, if
any. - Lighting or material changes. - End state. - Transition to the
next shot. - Text that must remain exact and readable.

Use concrete verbs and observable changes. Avoid vague directions such
as "make it cinematic," "add beautiful motion," or "make it dynamic"
without defining the actual behavior.

### Step 8 --- Build and animate

Work from the main idea outward: 1. Establish composition. 2. Animate
the primary action. 3. Set timing and easing. 4. Add secondary motion.
5. Integrate texture, shadows, and lighting. 6. Add typography and
labels. 7. Add sound and final polish.

Do not use effects to compensate for a weak concept or composition.

### Step 9 --- Quality assurance

Review the piece: - At full resolution. - At intended mobile size. - At
normal playback speed. - Frame by frame around transitions. - With audio
and, where relevant, without audio. - For spelling, shaping, safe
margins, and logo integrity. - For jumps, flicker, texture swimming,
inconsistent shadows, and accidental visual clutter.

### Step 10 --- Originality audit

Ask: - Does the central metaphor belong to this topic? - Are the shot
order and compositions independently designed? - Have any distinctive
motifs been copied too literally? - Would changing the palette still
leave the work recognizably derivative? - Does the piece have its own
movement signature? If it resembles a known work too closely, change the
visual metaphor, staging, composition, or transition structure---not
merely the colors.

## 10. Production-specific instructions

### For After Effects / compositing

-   Use precompositions and clear layer naming.
-   Separate texture from typography where possible.
-   Keep shadows editable.
-   Use 2.5D only where parallax or camera perspective adds value.
-   Use motion blur selectively and consistently.
-   Avoid stacking effects without a visible purpose.
-   Keep text editable and verify Arabic shaping in the final render.

### For Blender / 3D

-   Use real geometry only where shape, parallax, or lighting requires
    it.
-   Match focal length and camera movement to the composition.
-   Avoid unnecessary depth-of-field blur that harms text.
-   Keep physically based material behavior consistent.
-   Check shadow direction and contact with surfaces.
-   Do not create complex 3D assets merely to imitate a look that can be
    built more efficiently in 2D.

### For HTML Canvas / SVG / code-driven animation

-   Separate scene state, rendering, and timing logic.
-   Use deterministic time-based animation rather than frame-count-only
    logic.
-   Support seeking to arbitrary timestamps when the production pipeline
    requires it (for example, a `seek(t)` interface).
-   Keep typography, assets, and motion parameters editable.
-   Avoid random values that change on every render; seed or precompute
    procedural variation.
-   Maintain consistent pixel ratio, aspect ratio, and safe areas.
-   Test at scene boundaries and arbitrary timestamps to detect
    discontinuities.

### For AI video generation

-   Define the shot as one continuous, physically coherent action when
    that is the goal.
-   Specify camera position and movement, subject behavior, material
    response, lighting, and end state.
-   Preserve identity and supplied references when required.
-   Avoid conflicting motion instructions.
-   Do not ask the model to render complex, exact typography if it is
    likely to distort; create exact text in post-production when
    necessary.
-   Do not rely on generic words such as "premium," "cinematic," or
    "viral" as substitutes for visible instructions.
-   If continuity matters, describe the actual visible starting state
    and required end state; do not assume the model understands hidden
    production history.
-   Prefer a small number of clearly defined movements over a list of
    simultaneous effects.

## 11. Audio and timing

Motion and sound should share a rhythm, but they do not need to hit
every beat.

-   Use sound to support actions, reveals, transitions, and material
    cues.
-   Do not add a whoosh to every movement.
-   Preserve room tone, natural sound, voice, or other required audio.
-   Keep music beneath essential narration.
-   Align key transitions to meaningful audio events when it helps.
-   Let some moments breathe without a sound accent.
-   Check the mix on phone speakers and headphones.
-   Avoid using sound effects to make weak visual timing seem energetic.

## 12. Common failure modes and corrections

**Failure: Generic template look.**\
Correction: replace the default metaphor and composition with a concept
derived from the topic.

**Failure: Everything moves.**\
Correction: define one primary action per beat and freeze secondary
elements when they are not needed.

**Failure: Texture overload.**\
Correction: reduce texture contrast, vary texture scale, and preserve
clean zones for information.

**Failure: Fake depth.**\
Correction: align overlap, perspective, shadow, and scale; remove depth
cues that contradict each other.

**Failure: Random camera motion.**\
Correction: assign a narrative purpose to every camera move or keep the
camera still.

**Failure: Over-easing and bounce.**\
Correction: shorten overshoot, reduce amplitude, and use controlled
settling.

**Failure: Text is unreadable.**\
Correction: increase hold time, simplify background, reduce motion
during reading, and verify the final-size output.

**Failure: Effects overpower meaning.**\
Correction: remove any effect that does not improve comprehension,
hierarchy, material believability, or emotional intent.

**Failure: Copying a reference.**\
Correction: retain only high-level principles and rebuild the visual
metaphor, staging, palette, and scene order.

**Failure: One visual treatment applied to every subject.**\
Correction: select materials and movement based on the subject. The
skill is a quality framework, not a fixed visual preset.

## 13. Final delivery format for an agent

When asked to create a motion-graphics piece, deliver the relevant items
below according to the user's request:

1.  **Creative thesis:** one paragraph explaining the original visual
    idea.
2.  **Visual DNA:** palette roles, material logic, typography, depth,
    and lighting.
3.  **Beat sheet or storyboard:** ordered scenes with purpose and
    transitions.
4.  **Shot-level instructions:** duration, composition, primary
    movement, easing, secondary motion, and end state.
5.  **Asset list:** required files, layers, references, and exact text.
6.  **Implementation plan or prompts:** tailored to the requested tool.
7.  **Quality checklist:** readability, consistency, timing, physical
    coherence, and originality.

If the user asks only for a prompt, provide the prompt without an
unnecessary essay. If they ask for a storyboard only, provide storyboard
frames and scene actions without adding unrelated production notes.
Follow the requested deliverable while applying the principles
internally.

## 14. Pre-delivery scorecard

Score each category from 0 to 2: - Concept is original and
topic-specific. - Visual hierarchy is clear. - Materials are coherent
and appropriately scaled. - Camera movement has a narrative purpose. -
Easing matches movement intent and perceived weight. - Text is readable
and correct. - Background supports rather than competes with the
subject. - Lighting, shadows, and depth agree. - Scene transitions are
motivated. - Motion is restrained and not gratuitous. - Sound supports
rather than overwhelms the visual story. - The result does not reproduce
another work's distinctive expression.

**Target:** 20/24 or higher, with no zero in originality, readability,
or coherence. If below target, revise before presenting the result as
finished.

## 15. Governing principle

**Build a visual language, not a preset. Tell the idea through
composition and movement. Make materials believable, motion purposeful,
and every scene specific to the subject. Preserve the craft; reinvent
the expression.**
